How Campus Sentinel works

Syndromic surveillance watches for groups of symptoms before lab tests confirm a diagnosis. It trades certainty for speed: a health team hears about a stomach bug in one residence hall in days, not weeks.

  1. Collect. A student submits an anonymous report: campuses, residence, onset date, symptoms, severity, and exposures (shared meals, travel, sick contacts).
  2. Group into a syndrome. Symptoms are sorted into one syndrome. Influenza-like illness uses the CDC definition: fever of 100°F or higher plus cough or sore throat.
  3. Score pathogen type. Each rule below adds points to one or more categories. The category with the most points wins. Confidence is that category's share of all points: 60% or more is high, 40–59% is moderate, below 40% is low.
  4. Detect clusters. Reports from the last 7 days are counted by residence and syndrome. Three or more in one residence is a Watch. Five or more, or any meningitis-like report, is an Alert. The same syndrome at five or more students across one campus raises a campus-wide signal. A cluster is Rising when this week's count is at least double the week before.
  5. Recommend. Each cluster shows the pathogens to look for, based on its syndrome and its most common pathogen category.

Scoring rules

Limits

The patterns come from the sources in the table, but the point values are the author's judgment, not weights measured from lab-confirmed cases. Symptoms alone cannot confirm a pathogen. Self-reports can be duplicated or missing, and students who feel mildly sick report less often. Rules here are simplified teaching versions of clinical patterns. A real deployment would confirm clusters with lab testing and campus health staff.

Not medical advice. For trouble breathing, a stiff neck with fever, confusion, or chest pain, call 911. For other care, contact ASU Health Services or your own provider.
