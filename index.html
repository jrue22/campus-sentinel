How Campus Sentinel works

Syndromic surveillance watches for groups of symptoms before lab tests confirm a diagnosis. It trades certainty for speed: a health team hears about a stomach bug in one residence hall in days, not weeks.

  1. Collect. A student submits an anonymous report: campuses, residence, onset date, symptoms, severity, and exposures (shared meals, travel, sick contacts).
  2. Group into a syndrome. Symptoms are sorted into one syndrome. Influenza-like illness uses the CDC definition: fever of 100°F or higher plus cough or sore throat.
  3. Score pathogen type. Each rule below adds points to one or more categories. The category with the most points wins. Confidence is that category's share of all points: 60% or more is high, 40–59% is moderate, below 40% is low.
  4. Detect clusters. Reports from the last 7 days are counted by residence and syndrome. Three or more in one residence is a Watch. Five or more, or any meningitis-like report, is an Alert. The same syndrome at five or more students across one campus raises a campus-wide signal. A cluster is Rising when this week's count is at least double the week before.
  5. Recommend. Each cluster shows the pathogens to look for, based on its syndrome and its most common pathogen category.
  6. Cross-check with wastewater. Sewage samples measure virus and bacteria genes shed by everyone on a sewer line, including people who never report. For each site and target, the geometric mean (an average on a log scale) of the last 7 days is compared with the 28 days before. 2× baseline is elevated and 4× is high; a first detection after only non-detects is elevated. Values are divided by PMMoV, a virus every person sheds, when every sample in the series has it. The map shows each sampling site colored by its strongest signal. A wastewater signal that matches a symptom cluster (same syndrome, same place) is marked + wastewater. One with no matching cluster is a possible early warning.
  7. Measure lead time. The wastewater rise date is the first sample in the last 21 days at 2× baseline or more. Lead time is the number of days from that date to the first symptom onset in the matching cluster; positive means wastewater saw it first.
  8. Track SARS-CoV-2 lineages. Sequencing results (lineage shares from a tool like Freyja) are shown as a stacked bar per sample. Diversity is the Shannon index, H = −Σ p ln p, shown as eH, the effective number of lineages.

Scoring rules

Limits

The patterns come from the sources in the table, but the point values are the author's judgment, not weights measured from lab-confirmed cases. Symptoms alone cannot confirm a pathogen. Self-reports can be duplicated or missing, and students who feel mildly sick report less often. Rules here are simplified teaching versions of clinical patterns. A real deployment would confirm clusters with lab testing and campus health staff. Wastewater shows that a pathogen is present in a sewershed, not who is sick or how sick. Sewer lines can serve people outside the dorms, bacterial assays are far less standardized than viral ones, and the 2× and 4× thresholds are simple teaching values rather than percentile baselines like the CDC uses.

Not medical advice. For trouble breathing, a stiff neck with fever, confusion, or chest pain, call 911. For other care, contact ASU Health Services or your own provider.
